When I joined Appsilon as Head of Marketing, there was no marketing function. There was a team, sure — talented people doing good work. But there was no system. No shared model for why we did what we did. Content got published because it was someone's turn. Events happened because someone said yes to a booth. Leads came in — or didn't — and nobody could explain why.

I inherited what a lot of B2B marketers inherit: Random Acts of Marketing.

The team and its activities were optimized for passive motion — inbound and brand awareness — not for active pipeline creation. We relied entirely on organic inbound, which meant that when traffic dipped (and it did), there was no backup. No structured outbound. No performance-based KPIs. No predictability. If you asked "how many leads will marketing generate next quarter?" the honest answer was a shrug.

That's not a marketing team. That's a content studio hoping for the best.

## The diagnosis

Before I touched tactics, I needed to understand what was actually broken. Not symptoms — causes. A "we need more content" complaint is almost never a content problem. It's usually a positioning problem, or a targeting problem, or a "we have no idea who we're talking to" problem.

At Appsilon — a global B2B consultancy serving pharma, biotech, and data-heavy industries — the core issue was structural. Marketing wasn't organized around outcomes. It was organized around activities. People had tasks, not functions. There were no clear levers to pull when something went wrong, and no way to tell which efforts actually moved the needle.

So I stopped thinking about campaigns and started thinking about architecture.

## Three pillars

I restructured the entire marketing function around three pillars. Not because three is a magic number, but because these are the three things that, when separated and measured independently, let you actually diagnose what's working and what isn't.

### Brand — the fuel ⛽

Brand is everything that makes inbound possible. Thought leadership, case studies, conference talks, the website, visual identity, community presence. It's how you exist in the minds of the 95% of your market that is not currently buying.

Brand doesn't generate MQLs directly. That makes it the first thing that gets cut when leadership panics about pipeline — and the hardest thing to rebuild once it's gone. My job was to protect it while making it measurable. We defined brand KPIs: conference registrations, website traffic, content reach, share of voice. Not perfect proxies, but enough to show that the fuel tank wasn't empty.

In practice, this meant scaling case studies from a handful to over 30 in a single year. Publishing 100+ blog posts. Coordinating 51 talks across events on three continents. Running Shiny Conf — the biggest virtual event in our community — for four consecutive years, eventually reaching over 2,000 registrants. Organizing the first GxP Validation Summit, pulling in registrations from companies like Novartis, Merck, AstraZeneca, and Moderna.

None of that "generated a lead" in the CRM sense. All of it made the lead generation engine possible.

### Market Sensing — the radar 📡

This is the pillar most marketing teams skip entirely, and it's the one I'm most proud of building.

Market Sensing is your feedback loop with reality. It's how you know whether your positioning actually resonates, whether the market is shifting under your feet, and whether competitors are eating your lunch while you're busy writing blog posts.

At Appsilon, this meant attending industry events not just to present, but to listen. Customer interviews. Competitor tracking. Structured intelligence from every client-facing interaction. We built the first version of a competitive intelligence knowledge base. We ran market research campaigns around emerging categories where we saw opportunity.

The insight that changed everything for me: you cannot have world-class marketing if your team doesn't actually talk to the market. Sounds obvious, right? But I've watched entire marketing departments operate for years based on assumptions that nobody ever validated. Market Sensing is the function that prevents that.

It also enabled a tighter feedback loop between marketing and sales. When your market sensing is strong, your messaging stays current, your campaigns hit harder, and you stop wasting cycles on positioning that stopped being true six months ago.

### Lead Generation — the engine 🚀

This is where it all converts. Lead gen is the function aimed at the 5% of your market that is actually in-market right now. Inbound, outbound, ABM, paid — all of it lives here.

The biggest shift I made was moving from pure inbound dependency to an allbound model. Inbound is great when it works. But when Google changes its algorithm (and it will), or when AI Overviews start eating your organic traffic (and they did), you need other channels that are not subject to a platform's whims.

We built a prospecting engine where marketing-generated warm contacts fed into structured sales outreach lists. We implemented HubSpot end-to-end for pipeline visibility. We set up and managed agency partnerships for outbound, with clear expectations and accountability. We delivered the best marketing year in the company's history in terms of qualified lead generation — and for the first time, marketing could actually predict its output.

## Why three and not four, or seven

Every marketing framework I've seen either collapses everything into "awareness → conversion" (too simple to be useful) or explodes into twelve functions with a hundred sub-KPIs (too complex to actually operate).

Three pillars worked because they map to three different time horizons and three different conversations with leadership:

- **Brand** is the long game. "Are we building something that compounds?"
- **Market Sensing** is the present tense. "Do we actually understand what the market needs right now?"
- **Lead Gen** is the short game. "Are we generating pipeline this quarter?"

When pipeline dips, you can diagnose: is it a brand problem (nobody knows us), a sensing problem (we're talking about the wrong things), or an engine problem (the mechanics of conversion are broken)? Without the separation, everything looks like "marketing isn't working" and the response is always "write more blog posts," which is almost never the real answer.

## What it looked like in practice

I'm not going to pretend the transformation was clean. It took the better part of two years. Some things I got right early — hiring a senior marketing manager who immediately elevated strategy and execution was probably the best decision I made. Other things took longer. Getting the team to think in functions rather than tasks required months of coaching, new processes, and a lot of patience from people who were used to a different way of working.

We introduced SCRUM to marketing — actual sprints, standups, retros. It improved shipping cadence dramatically. We built a content pipeline that moved us from "who wants to write something this week?" to planned, structured production. We created an events playbook that standardized how we selected, prepared for, and followed up on conferences.

By the end, we had result-based Areas of Responsibility with health metrics for each function. Green, yellow, red — you could look at the dashboard and know where to focus. We had a marketing manual — a real one, not a slide deck — that any new hire could read and understand how the whole machine worked.

The before-and-after still surprises me when I look at it. From no documented strategy to a three-pillar model with clear KPIs. From sporadic blog posts to 100+ articles a year with a structured pipeline. From unstructured event attendance to a playbook that drove 51 talks and 10 webinars. From engineering-dependent WordPress to a Webflow site the marketing team fully owned.

## What I'd do differently

I'd build Market Sensing first, not last. In practice, I stood up Brand and Lead Gen before I had a proper sensing function, which meant early campaigns were based on assumptions I hadn't validated yet. Some of them were wrong. If I had the radar running from day one, the engine would have been more efficient from the start.

I'd also invest earlier in making the model legible to non-marketers. The three pillars made perfect sense to the marketing team, but it took too long for sales and leadership to internalize the framework. A simpler internal comms cadence — here's what each pillar did this month, here's what it means for pipeline — would have built alignment faster.

## Where this is going

Here's the thing that excites me now. The operating system I built at Appsilon was manual. Every campaign brief, every competitive analysis, every content plan — written by hand, coordinated through project management tools and Slack threads and Google Docs.

But the model itself — context in, campaigns out — is begging to be automated.

I've been building tools that do exactly that. A marketing OS that stores strategy, positioning, brand rules, and ICP data in a version-controlled repo — and uses AI to generate campaign briefs, draft copy, and assemble assets that are downstream of governed context. Not "AI writes your marketing." More like: the system remembers everything you've decided, and the AI operates within those guardrails.

The market sensing pillar is where it gets really interesting. Competitive intelligence that runs continuously — scraping competitor sites, tracking positioning changes, monitoring feature releases. Or querying search engines and communities to audit how your market actually talks about the problems you solve, and comparing that against your own positioning. That gap between how you describe yourself and how the market describes the problem? That's where the best marketing insights live. And now you can surface them programmatically instead of waiting for someone to stumble across them at a conference.

I'm not building this because I think AI replaces marketers. I'm building it because I've lived the alternative — the manual operating system, the tribal knowledge, the "I know it's in a Google Doc somewhere." The three-pillar model works. It just works better when the system can remember, reason, and move faster than any team can on its own.
