**

This (opinionated) essay is a high-level, non-exhaustive summary about what good marketing looks like to me. It is not a one-size-fits-all solution to all your problems, nor is it the one and only way to do good marketing. It is, however, a good starting point to understanding how complex marketing can be, and how to think about good B2B marketing in tech. 
## Introduction

When I took over Appsilon's Marketing, there was no marketing function. There was a team, sure, and a very well working one — talented people doing good work with some data to back that up. But there was no system, or a shared model for why we did what we did. Content got published because it was someone's turn, or maybe some software engineer felt like sharing their learnings. What worked and brought results did so without the team understanding why it worked, or how to replicate it. Same went for things that didn't work. Leads came in, or didn't and nobody could explain why.

I inherited what a lot of B2B marketers inherit: Random Acts of Marketing. An engine that runs, but doesn't have a manual, isn't optimized for its purpose and hasn't been maintained for a while.

The team and its activities were set up for passive motion --> inbound and brand awareness, missing any active pipeline creation. We relied entirely on organic inbound, which meant that when market conditions changed (and it did) or traffic dipped (and it sure did!), there was no backup. No structured outbound or nurture, or any direct and active line of communication with our community. No predictability. If you asked "how many leads will marketing generate next quarter?" the honest answer was a shrug. OK, let's be real, modelling leads volume in enterprise services with a crazy long sales cycle and primarily inbound-led pipeline is always going to be a guesstimate. But being able to project the number of webinar subscriptions, ebook downloads, website traffic, conversion rates between all the different stages and so on, is not only super helpful but also critically important.

Without these and more, you don't have a marketing team but a content studio hoping for the best.
## OK, Sounds Great, Where do I Start?

So how does this work in practice? Before I touch anything, I need to understand what is broken and what is working well and why. A "we need more content" complaint is almost never a content problem, and rarely makes sense. Unless all your content turns to gold (which is possible, but highly unlikely :)) you don't need more content, but the right content for the right audience that reaches them and at the right time. It's usually a positioning problem, or a targeting problem, or a "we have no idea who we're talking to" problem.

At Appsilon (a global B2B consultancy serving pharma, biotech, and data-heavy industries) the core issue was structural. Marketing wasn't organized around outcomes. It was organized around activities. People had tasks, not functions. There were no clear levers to pull when something went wrong, and no way to tell which efforts actually moved the needle.

Fixing a problem like this one is starts with a simple conviction about what good B2B marketing actually is. And it's two things: a deep, current understanding of the market, and a clear, consistent brand. You must know what value you deliver and for whom, then putting that in front of the right people at the right time. Sounds very simple but rarely is.

Content is how you carry it; in this business content is king and queen. And marketing sits at the base of the growth stack: it builds the awareness and trust that sales converts into customers and account management grows. 

Optimizing individual activities is very tempting. You get to do stuff and you see results fast, but it just compounds the problem. You need to srart designing the architecture underneath the problem.

## The Three Pillars

It's a simplification but a good marketing function can be built around three pillars that let you diagnose what's working and what isn't, and when done right give you structured and predictable growth. Important caveat --> this assumes you have a strong value proposition and offer/product & market fit. With good offering you can and will grow without marketing, but with poor offering even the world's best marketing team will struggle to grow the business. 

### Brand = the fuel ⛽

Brand is everything that makes inbound (and outbound) possible. Community, thought leadership, case studies, conference talks, the website, visual identity, community presence. It's how you exist in the minds of the 95% of your market that is not currently buying, and what convinces the 5% that are in market and in buying mode. [Typically, only around 3% to 5% of your market are "in-market" at any given time](https://www.linkedin.com/posts/dalewharrison_the-95-5-rule-95-of-your-potential-buyers-share-7099099204295266304-7XZf/) (and no, you can't make the 5% into 20% by "creating demand", and if someone tells you they will do it, they are lying to you :)).

Side note: brand doesn't generate Leads/MQLs/SQLs directly. That makes it the first thing that gets cut when leadership panics about pipeline, and the hardest thing to rebuild once it's gone. 

A practical example and what it meant for me: scaling case studies from a handful to over 30 in a single year, to give buyers proof of value you deliver. Publishing 100+ blogposts to bring traffic to the website. Delivering webinars that aren't sales pitches, and that attract the proper persona from companies you want to sell to and more.

None of that "generates a lead" in the CRM sense. All of it makes the lead generation engine possible, builds trust with your community (or starts the community building process) and gives your sales team a direction.

### Market Intelligence = your radar 📡

This is the pillar most marketing teams skip entirely, and it's  one you must build and maintain, from market sensing, through customer and offer development to competetive intelligence. 

This is your feedback loop with reality. It's how you know whether your positioning works, whether the market is shifting under your feet, and whether competitors are eating your lunch while you're  writing blogposts noone wants to read anymore.

This can mean many things: attending industry events not just to present, but to listen, customer interviews, competitor tracking, capturing and reflecting on insights from every client-facing interaction (talk with your sales team!). At Appsilon we ran market research campaigns around emerging categories where we saw opportunity.

You cannot have world-class marketing if your team doesn't actually talk to the market. Sounds obvious, right? But I've watched entire marketing departments operate for years based on assumptions that nobody ever validated. 

### Lead Generation = the engine 🚀

This is where it all converts. Lead gen is the function aimed at the 5% of your market that is actually in-market right now. Inbound, outbound, ABM, paid; all of it lives here.

Some companies grow through outbound (sales-led, founder-led), and some through inbound. A holy grail is a an allbound model, with a good mix of leads coming in through all the channels. Inbound is great when it works. But when Google changes its algorithm (and it has, and it certainly will again), or when AI Overviews start eating your organic traffic (and they did), you need other channels that are not subject to a platform's whims.

You need both, an inbound motion that means being able to be found by your clients on the internet (and being top of mind when they look for solution you offer), but also a way build and run a prospecting engine where marketing-generated warm contacts are fed into structured sales outreach lists. 

These pillars behave like connected vessels: brand makes the lead-gen engine more effective, underastanding the market sharpens both the brand and the engine, and lead gen capitalizes on the foundation the other two lay down. 

## Why three and not four, or seven

There's as many marketing frameworks as there are marketers, and most of them can work given the right circumstance. I think a "awareness → conversion" model is too simple to be useful but managing twelve functions with a hundred sub-KPIs is too complex to actually operate. Doesn't matter the size of your org.

These pillars work because they map to three different time horizons and three different conversations with leadership:

- **Brand** is the long game. "Are we building something that compounds?"
- **Market Intelligence** is the present tense. "Do we actually understand what the market needs right now?"
- **Lead Gen** is the short game. "Are we generating pipeline this quarter?"

When pipeline dips, you can diagnose: is it a brand problem (nobody knows us), a problem with understanding what the market wants or having what the market wants (we're talking about the wrong things, or we simply don't have what the market wants), or an engine problem (the mechanics of conversion are broken)? Without the separation, everything looks like "marketing isn't working" and the response is always "write more blog posts" or "replace the marketing team, that'll fix that surely!" which is almost never the real answer.

## What it Looked Like in Practice

Doing it right is not easy or fast. The first time around it took me the better part of two years and I stumbled and failed many times over along the way. Some things I got right early e.g. hiring amazing people who immediately elevate strategy and execution. Other things took longer. Getting the team to think in functions rather than tasks required months of coaching, new processes, and a lot of patience from people who were used to a different way of working, and sometimes letting people go.

## The Future is Bright

Here's the thing that excites me most today. The system I describe here I had to build manually at Appsion. Every campaign brief, plan,  competitive analysis, every content plan, landing page... written by hand, coordinated through project management tools and Slack threads and Google Docs.

But the model itself = context in, campaigns out, is begging to be automated. You need to build the context and critically think about what you put in, but the blueprint is in many ways very repeatable.

AI got really good in recent years. Deceptively good. There are risks, and many people misuse AI, outsourcing critical thinkiung to the machine (which is exactly the opposite of wehat they should be doing! see [How to Use AI the Right Way](lorem ipsum)). 

I've been building tools that do exactly that. A marketing system that stores strategy, positioning, brand rules, and ICP data in a version-controlled repo and uses AI to generate campaign briefs, draft copy, and assemble assets that are downstream of governed context. Not "AI writes your marketing." More like: the system remembers everything you've decided, and the AI operates within those guardrails.

I don't think AI replaces marketers. But it helps avopind the alternative = the manual system, the "tribal" knowledge, the "I know it's in a Google Doc somewhere", or the "I need to spend 2 days creating a campaign plan and 90% of the work is the same as last time".
